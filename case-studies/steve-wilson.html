01 The problem

What makes someone want to move from a first impression into the work?

A portfolio has to communicate what a designer does, offer a convincing sample of their work, and make contact straightforward. This early concept uses the fictional Steve Wilson identity to explore that sequence through a home page, work listing, project-detail template, and contact destination.

Designed for Prospective client reviewing a portfolio. Understand the designer’s offer and inspect a suitable example.

User context Design assumption

Scanning for relevant work before deciding whether to make contact.

The friction
A polished introduction alone does not explain the work.
Design implication
Move quickly from identity to selected projects.

Example situation · illustrative scenario. Picture a reviewer opening a designer’s portfolio between other applications. The first page has to establish what the designer does and make one relevant project easy to choose.

02 The outcome

A strong front door, with depth still to add.

I produced a visual portfolio concept with a work index, reusable project-detail structure, and contact destination. It demonstrates an early attempt to make a portfolio readable at both scanning and deep-reading levels. The fictional identity, sample client attribution, and numerical placeholders belong to the template, not my professional record.

More about the experience
  • A type-led opening. The name treatment establishes the visual entry point.
  • A separate work index. Projects can be scanned before opening the longer story.

03 Research & direction

The useful signal

The original concept already separates browsing from deep reading. Its project template creates room for a story, but that room still needs real decisions and evidence.

My direction. I used the retrospective to connect the visual hierarchy to the reviewer’s sequence: orient, select, investigate, then make contact.

More about the original process

The available evidence is a four-page visual artifact, including a long project-detail template. It contains sample copy, mock project names, placeholder results, and a sample client attribution. Those are layout content, not my client history or achieved results.

For this retrospective I evaluated the visible structure: the introduction establishes the designer’s role, the work listing exposes project categories and tools, and the detail template reserves space for the problem, process, outcome, and lessons. No original research or testing record accompanies it.

04 Design decisions

Lead from the work into the case study.

Open a decision to see the reasoning and trade-off.

01

Use typography to establish the first impression

+

The concept combines a large name treatment with a restrained dark background and a bright accent.

Why this matters. The visual hierarchy gives the identity a clear point of entry before visitors scan services or projects.

The trade-off. A strong name treatment cannot replace useful positioning copy; I would make the designer’s particular value more specific.

02

Separate browsing from deep reading

+

The work listing acts as an index, while project details have a longer narrative structure.

Why this matters. Visitors can skim the portfolio before deciding which story deserves more attention.

The trade-off. A listing only helps if its labels explain the work rather than relying on unfamiliar project names.

03

Give each project the same narrative spine

+

The template includes role, problem, process, results, takeaways, and final product.

Why this matters. A repeated structure helps a reviewer compare contributions across projects.

The trade-off. In the archive, much of that structure is empty sample content. A template is useful scaffolding, not evidence of a completed case study.

05 Reflection

The layout earns attention; the evidence earns confidence.

The retrospective lesson is directly relevant to my portfolio today: a convincing case study needs specific decisions and defensible evidence inside the layout. Attractive headings cannot do that work on their own.

What I would evaluate next proposed work
  • replace the sample project with one real story, then test whether a reviewer can identify the role, central decision, and outcome in a short scan.
  • verify contrast, keyboard navigation, responsive reading order, and a concise contact path before implementation.
