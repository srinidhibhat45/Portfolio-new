01 The problem

What does the group need to understand at every handoff?

A short team game loses momentum when people are unsure how to join or whose turn it is. The reviewed Office Games module centres on a simple room flow, a lobby, team roles, and a visible answering/stealing sequence.

Designed for Colleague joining a short group game. Join quickly and know whose turn it is.

User context Design assumption

Entering a room and learning a team role during an office break.

The friction
Waiting and role changes become confusing when the interface is silent.
Design implication
Make room, role, and next action visible.

Example situation · illustrative scenario. Imagine a short team break being spent explaining how to join, who can answer, and why the game is waiting. The social rhythm depends on those transitions being obvious.

02 The outcome

A room with a shared social rhythm.

The reviewed trivia module connects joining, teams, round state, scoring, and chat. My UX focus makes the social sequence and responsibility for the next action visible. Local demonstration and connected play need distinct expectations.

More about the experience
  • A social entry point. The product offers a quick route into a shared game.
  • Reviewed scope: trivia. Local demo and network-backed play make different connection promises.

03 The direction

Someone needs the next turn

Shared state has to become shared understanding. Room entry, team roles, and the answering/stealing sequence all contribute to the pace.

Product artifact · reviewed trivia module

My direction. I focused the reviewed trivia module on a concise joining path and visible responsibility through each round.

Process and product scope

I mapped the reviewed trivia journey from joining a room to understanding teams, turns, answering, and stealing. The broader games catalogue is outside the evidence available for this case study.

The UX needs to explain the connection mode before a group commits to a game. Trying a local demonstration and joining colleagues on separate devices are different experiences.

04 Design decisions

Make the next turn clear to everyone.

Open a decision to see the reasoning and trade-off.

01

Make joining a small action

+

A four-letter room code and a player name provide a concise entry path.

Why this matters. A social break should spend more of its time playing than explaining setup.

The trade-off. A short code still needs clear invalid-room and full-room handling, and does not establish secure identity.

02

Expose responsibility in the game state

+

The interface distinguishes answering and stealing teams, active players, and scores.

Why this matters. People need to see who can act and why the round is waiting.

The trade-off. Role-based interaction becomes confusing if waiting states or connection loss are not explained.

03

Keep connection mode understandable

+

The setup distinguishes a local demonstration from connected play.

Why this matters. A demo can help someone try the game, but should not promise multi-device play it cannot provide.

The trade-off. A connected game needs clear waiting, conflict, reconnection, and host-recovery states.

05 Reflection

A social game moves when everyone knows what happens next.

The design opportunity is a shared understanding of what happens next. I would assess joining, waiting, and recovery as carefully as the answer-selection screen.

What I would evaluate next proposed work
  • run a short trivia session with several participants and record setup explanations, confused turns, and waiting time.
  • separate-device joining, reconnection, duplicate actions, host departure, and a clearly labelled local demo mode.
